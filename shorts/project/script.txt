A drugstore in the middle of nowhere got rich by giving away water for free.
In nineteen thirty-one, a young pharmacist buys the only drugstore in Wall, South Dakota.
The town is so small, the tumbleweeds outnumber the customers.
He and his wife Dorothy give themselves five years to make it work.
Night and day, cars rumble past toward Mount Rushmore, and not one stops.
Four and a half years later, they're almost out of time.
Then, on a blazing summer day, Dorothy figures it out.
Those drivers don't need a drugstore. They need a drink.
So she writes a sign for the highway: free ice water.
Before her husband even gets back from putting it up, cars are already turning in.
You see, it's the same trick as free samples at the grocery store.
They come for the water and leave with ice cream, sodas, and souvenirs.
So they add more signs, then more, until they stretch hundreds of miles.
In World War Two, soldiers put up signs in Paris and Egypt.
One even shows up at the South Pole, over nine thousand miles away.
Back home, the tiny store grows until it swallows the whole block.
Today, up to twenty thousand people stop there on a summer day.
And the ice water? It's still free.
The coffee costs five cents.
