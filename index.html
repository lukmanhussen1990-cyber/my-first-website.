Meet the tiny living things all around you

Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. They make bread rise, keep us healthy and clean our planet. Some can also make us sick. Ready to explore their world?

01 The big idea

What are microorganisms?

A microorganism is a living thing that is too small to see with just your eyes. We also call it a microbe. my-kro-OR-guh-niz-um

microvery, very small
+
organisma living thing
=
microorganisma tiny living thing
🔬

Too small to see

Most microbes are thousands of times smaller than an ant. You need a microscope to see them. Thousands of them could sit on the full stop at the end of this sentence.

🌱

They are alive

Just like you, microbes eat, grow, make babies and react to the world around them. Most of them are made of just one cell.

🌍

They are everywhere

Microbes live in air, water, soil and food. They live on your skin and inside your body. Some even live in boiling hot springs and in ice.

38 trillion
bacteria live in and on your body. That is about the same as the number of your own body cells!
3.5 billion years
Microbes have lived on Earth for a very, very long time. That is long before the dinosaurs.
50%
About half of the oxygen we breathe is made by tiny microbes in the sea, like algae.
20 minutes
Some bacteria can double their number every 20 minutes when things are perfect for them.
👍

Good news! Most microbes are harmless or even helpful. Only a small number make us ill. We call those ones germs.

    03 Meet the family

    Five groups of microbes

    Scientists put microbes into five main groups. Pick a group to see how it looks and what it does.

    Bacteria The tiny one-cell builders

    Bacteria are microbes made of a single cell. They live almost everywhere. Some make us ill, but many are very helpful.

    Size
    About 0.5 to 5 µm. About 1,000 in a row would fit across a pin head.
    Body
    One cell with no true nucleus. The DNA floats freely inside.
    Moving
    Many swim with a whip-like tail called a flagellum.
    Babies
    A bacterium splits into two. This is called binary fission.
    👍 Helpful: curd bacteria, soil bacteria👎 Harmful: cholera, typhoid, TB

    💡 Your gut is home to trillions of bacteria. Many of them help you digest your food!

    Viruses The tiny hijackers

    Viruses are the smallest microbes of all. A virus is not a cell. It is just a set of genes inside a coat of protein.

    Size
    About 20 to 300 nm. That is 10 to 100 times smaller than a bacterium.
    Alive?
    On the edge of life! A virus cannot eat, grow or copy itself alone. It needs a living host cell.
    Babies
    It gets inside a host cell and tricks the cell into making lots of new viruses.
    Medicine
    Antibiotics do not work on viruses. Vaccines help to stop many virus diseases.
    👍 Helpful: phages kill harmful bacteria👎 Harmful: flu, measles, polio, dengue

    💡 A teaspoon of sea water can hold millions of viruses!

    Fungi Yeasts and moulds

    Fungi cannot make their own food. They feed on other things, like bread, fruit and dead leaves.

    Size
    Yeast cells are about 5 to 10 µm. Moulds grow long threads (hyphae) that make fuzzy patches you can see.
    Body
    Real cells with a nucleus and a tough cell wall. Yeast has one cell. Moulds have many.
    Feeding
    They give out juices that break food down. Then they soak up the food.
    Babies
    Yeast grows buds. Moulds make tiny spores that float in the air.
    👍 Helpful: bread yeast, penicillin mould👎 Harmful: bread mould, ringworm

    💡 The mould Penicillium gave us penicillin, the first antibiotic medicine.

    Protozoa The tiny hunters

    Protozoa are single-celled microbes that act like tiny animals. They hunt and eat other microbes.

    Size
    About 10 to 200 µm. Some are just big enough to see as a speck.
    Body
    One cell with a nucleus. Shapes can be blobs, slippers and many more.
    Moving
    Amoebas push out pseudopods (“false feet”). Paramecium rows with tiny hairs called cilia.
    Home
    Ponds, puddles, wet soil, and inside animals.
    👍 Helpful: eat bacteria in soil and water👎 Harmful: malaria, amoebic dysentery

    💡 Malaria is caused by a protozoan called Plasmodium. Female Anopheles mosquitoes carry it between people.

    Algae The tiny food-makers

    Algae are plant-like microbes. They use sunlight to make their own food, so they live in water and damp places.

    Size
    From a few µm for single cells, to long chains you can see, like green pond scum.
    Body
    Cells with a nucleus and green chloroplasts. Some are single cells. Others form chains.
    Food
    Photosynthesis: sunlight + water + carbon dioxide → sugar (food) + oxygen.
    Home
    Ponds, rivers, oceans, damp walls, even snow.
    👍 Helpful: make oxygen, feed fish👎 Harmful: too many make green “blooms”

    💡 “Blue-green algae” are really bacteria (cyanobacteria). Some help rice fields by adding nitrogen to the soil.

    04 The good guys

    Microbes that help us

    Most microbes are our friends! They make our food, help make medicines, feed the soil and clean up our planet. Here are six ways microbes help every day.

    🥛

    Curd and yoghurt

    Milk-loving bacteria turn the sugar in milk into acid. The milk gets thick and sour. Add one spoon of curd to warm milk, leave it overnight, and you get more curd!

    Lactobacillus · bacteria
    🍞

    Bread, idli and dosa

    Yeast eats sugar and makes a gas called carbon dioxide. The tiny gas bubbles make dough puff up and turn soft and fluffy. Idli and dosa batter rises in the same way.

    Yeast · a fungus
    💊

    Medicines

    The mould Penicillium makes penicillin, a medicine that kills bacteria. Microbes also help to make vaccines, and even insulin for people with diabetes.

    Penicillium · a mould
    🌱

    Healthy soil

    Rhizobium bacteria live in the roots of pea, bean and gram plants. They change nitrogen gas from the air into food for the plant. This is called nitrogen fixation.

    Rhizobium · bacteria
    ♻️

    Nature’s clean-up crew

    Decomposers, like bacteria and fungi, rot dead plants and animals. They turn waste into food for new plants. Other microbes clean dirty water at sewage plants.

    Decomposers
    🍽️

    Friends in your tummy

    Good bacteria in your gut help you digest food and make some vitamins. They also take up space so that harmful germs cannot settle in.

    Gut bacteria

    05 The bad guys

    Microbes that make us ill

    A few microbes cause diseases. We call them germs or pathogens. When germs get into our body and multiply, it is called an infection. Pick a type of microbe to see the diseases it causes.

    Diseases caused by germs

    How do germs travel?

    🌬️

    Air

    Coughs and sneezes send tiny drops into the air. Cover your mouth and nose when you cough.

    💧

    Water

    Dirty water can carry germs. Drink water that is boiled or treated.

    🍽️

    Food

    Unwashed hands and flies can leave germs on food. Cover food and cook it well.

    🦟

    Insects

    Mosquitoes and flies carry germs from place to place. We call them carriers.

    🤝

    Touch

    Germs pass from hand to hand and onto shared things. Wash your hands often.

    🍞 Why does food go bad?

    Microbes also live on our food. When they grow, bread gets mouldy, milk turns sour and fruit rots. We can slow them down or stop them. This is called food preservation.

    • ❄️
      Keep it coldA fridge slows microbes down. It does not kill them.
    • 🔥
      Cook or boil itHeat kills many microbes.
    • 🧂
      Add saltUsed for pickles and dried fish. Salt pulls water out of microbes.
    • 🍯
      Add lots of sugarJams and squash keep well because of the sugar.
    • ☀️
      Dry itMicrobes need water. Raisins and dal last for months.
    • 🥛
      Pasteurise itMilk is heated to about 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooled fast. Louis Pasteur invented this idea.

    🌾 Plants and animals get ill too

    Microbes do not only attack people. They can harm crops and farm animals, too.

    • 🍊
      Citrus cankerBacteria · rough brown spots on citrus leaves and fruit
    • 🌾
      Rust of wheatFungus · orange-brown powder on the leaves
    • 🌿
      Yellow vein mosaic of okraVirus · spread by whiteflies
    • 🐄
      AnthraxBacteria · harms cattle and sometimes people
    • 🐮
      Foot-and-mouth diseaseVirus · harms cattle

    06 Growing fast

    One becomes millions

    Bacteria make new bacteria by splitting in two. One cell becomes two, two become four, four become eight… In perfect conditions, some bacteria can split every 20 minutes. Let’s watch it happen!

    1 One becomes two

    A bacterium does not have parents and babies like we do. When it is big enough, it copies its DNA and then splits in the middle. Now there are two bacteria! This is called binary fission.

    1. 1One bacterium, with one copy of its DNA
    2. 2It copies its DNA and grows longer
    3. 3The middle pinches in
    4. 4It splits. Two new bacteria!

    🍲

    Food spoils quickly

    Food left out in a warm place gives microbes a perfect home. In a few hours there can be millions of them. That is why we cover food and keep it cool.

    🤒

    Infections can grow fast

    A few germs that get into a cut can multiply quickly. Clean a wound early. Wash your hands so fewer germs get in.

    🧊

    Cold slows them down

    Microbes grow slowest when it is cold. A fridge cannot kill them, but it slows them down so food stays fresh for longer.

    🌍

    Mind-blowing maths: if nothing ever stopped it, one bacterium splitting every 20 minutes could make a pile as heavy as the whole Earth in about two days! Luckily this never happens. Food and space run out, and the bacteria slow down.

    07 Tiny hijackers

    How a virus makes copies

    A virus cannot make copies of itself alone. It has to get inside a living cell and trick the cell into doing the work. Step through the story to see how.

    Step 1 of 6

    Meet the virus

      🤔

      Are viruses alive?

      Scientists still argue about it! A virus does not eat, grow or make its own energy. But it has genes and it can change over time. Many people say viruses are “on the edge of life”.

      🚫

      Antibiotics do not work

      Antibiotics attack parts that bacteria have, like their cell walls. Viruses do not have those parts. So antibiotics cannot cure a cold or the flu. Use them only when a doctor says so.

      💉

      What does work?

      Vaccines teach your body to fight a virus before it can make you ill. Rest, drinking water and special antiviral medicines can also help. Your immune system is the real hero. Meet it next!

      08 Your body’s army

      Your body fights back

      You are surrounded by germs, yet you are not ill most of the time. Why? Your body has a clever defence team called the immune system. It works day and night without you noticing.

      1
      🧱

      Strong walls

      Your skin is a waterproof wall. Tears, spit and the sticky mucus in your nose trap germs. Stomach acid destroys many of the germs you swallow.

      2
      ⚔️

      Germ-eating soldiers

      If germs get in, white blood cells rush to the spot. Some of them swallow germs whole and destroy them. Redness and swelling show that the battle has started.

      3
      🧠

      A memory for germs

      Special white blood cells make antibodies. These stick to a germ like a name tag so it can be found and destroyed. Your body remembers the germ and reacts faster next time.

      💡 In real life you have millions of white blood cells. Some eat germs, some make antibodies and some destroy infected cells. They work as a team!

      How do vaccines work?

      1. 💉

        1 · A safe “practice” germ

        A vaccine holds a harmless piece of a germ, or a weakened germ. It cannot make you ill.

      2. ➜
      3. 🥋

        2 · Your body practises

        Your white blood cells meet the practice germ. They make antibodies and remember what the germ looks like.

      4. ➜
      5. 🛡️

        3 · Ready for the real thing

        If the real germ ever arrives, your body knows it at once and fights back fast, often before you feel ill.

      🌟

      Vaccines changed the world. Smallpox once killed millions of people. Thanks to vaccines, the World Health Organization announced in 1980 that smallpox was gone from the Earth for ever.

      09 Microbe heroes

      The people who found the tiny world

      For thousands of years, nobody knew why people got ill. Then a few curious people looked closely, asked questions and changed the world. Here are some of them.

      Timeline of discoveries

      1. about 900📜

        Al-Razi

        Persian doctor

        Wrote a famous book that told smallpox and measles apart for the first time. Careful notes like his are how medicine learns.

      2. 1670s🔍

        Antonie van Leeuwenhoek

        Dutch lens-maker

        Built tiny, very powerful microscopes by hand. He was the first person to see bacteria and protozoa. He called them “animalcules”, which means tiny animals.

      3. 1796🐄

        Edward Jenner

        English doctor

        Used cowpox to protect a boy from deadly smallpox. This was the first vaccine. The word “vaccine” comes from vacca, the Latin word for cow.

      4. 1860s🧪

        Louis Pasteur

        French scientist

        Showed that microbes make food go bad and cause disease. He invented pasteurisation and made vaccines, including one for rabies.

      5. 1867🩺

        Joseph Lister

        British surgeon

        Cleaned wounds and surgical tools with a germ-killing liquid. Far fewer patients died after operations. Clean hospitals began here.

      6. 1876–1882🔬

        Robert Koch

        German doctor

        Proved that one particular germ causes one particular disease. He found the bacteria behind anthrax (1876) and tuberculosis (1882).

      7. 1892–1898🌿

        Ivanovsky & Beijerinck

        Russian and Dutch scientists

        Found that something even smaller than bacteria could make tobacco plants ill. It slipped through the finest filters. It was the first virus ever discovered.

      8. 1928💊

        Alexander Fleming

        Scottish doctor

        Noticed that a blue-green mould had killed the bacteria around it on a dish he had left out. This led to penicillin, the first antibiotic.

      9. 1980🌍

        The whole world

        World Health Organization

        Announced that smallpox was gone from the Earth. It is the first human disease ever wiped out, thanks to vaccines and teamwork.

      10 Stay safe

      Wash those germs away

      Washing your hands with soap is one of the best ways to stop germs from spreading. Soap pulls germs off your skin so that water can wash them down the drain. Let us practise the five steps!

      Germs left 100% Scrub time 0 / 20 s

      3. 3ScrubRub for 20 seconds

      Six healthy habits

      🧼

      Wash your hands

      Before you eat, after using the toilet, after coughing or sneezing, and after playing outside.

      🚰

      Drink safe water

      Drink clean water. If you are not sure, boil it or treat it first.

      🤧

      Cover coughs and sneezes

      Use a tissue or your elbow, not your hands. Put the tissue in a bin.

      🥗

      Keep food safe

      Wash fruit and vegetables. Cover food. Cook meat well. Keep leftovers cool.

      💉

      Get your vaccines

      They train your body to fight serious diseases before you meet them.

      🦟

      No standing water

      Empty old buckets and pots so mosquitoes cannot breed. Sleep under a net if you can.
