What is money?

Not the paper, not the metal, not the number on a screen. Money is an agreement: anything a whole society accepts in exchange for anything else.

Chapter 1Before money

Why barter breaks down

Imagine a village with no money at all. You can only swap what you have for what you want. Watch what happens when three people try.

A fisher has fish and wants bread. A baker has bread and wants shoes. A cobbler has shoes and wants fish. Everyone owns something useful, yet no two of them can trade.

Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. For a swap to work, I must want what you have and you must want what I have, at the same moment, in matching amounts.

Money cuts the knot. If everyone will accept one thing, a coin, say, then each person only needs to find someone who wants what they sell. One impossible three-way swap becomes three easy sales.

Chapter 2What money does

Three jobs, one object

Economists test anything that claims to be money against three jobs. Real money does all three at once.

Job 1

Medium of exchange

Something everyone takes as payment, so you never need to find a trading partner who wants exactly what you have.

Job 2

Unit of account

A common yardstick. Prices in one unit let you compare a coffee with a pair of shoes, add up a shopping list, or keep accounts.

Job 3

Store of value

Work today, spend next year. Money carries value through time, as long as prices stay reasonably stable.

Chapter 3What makes good money

The six-point test

Many things have served as money. The ones that lasted share six traits. Pick a candidate and run it through the test bench.

5.5out of 6 for gold coin

Durable

Does it last?Yes

Gold does not rust, rot or tarnish. Coins from Lydia still shine.

Portable

Easy to carry?Yes

Dense value: a pocketful of coins buys a cart of goods.

Divisible

Splits into small units?Yes

Mints strike coins of many weights, from a stater to a tiny fraction.

Uniform

Is every unit alike?Yes

A minted coin promises a fixed weight and purity, so no one has to assay it.

Scarce

Hard to make more?Yes

All the gold ever mined would fit in roughly four Olympic pools.

Accepted

Will everyone take it?Partly

Valued everywhere, but today's shops want cash or cards, not metal.

Chapter 4A short history

From cattle to code

Money has changed shape many times, but every form was a solution to the same matching problem. Scroll the timeline or use the arrows.

c. 9000 BCE
c. 9000 BCE

Cattle and grain

Farming settles people down, and herds and sacks of barley become the first commodity money. Latin pecunia, money, comes from pecus, cattle.

c. 3000 BCE

Ledgers in clay

Mesopotamian temples record debts in barley and silver on clay tablets. The shekel starts life as a weight, not a coin. Money is already a record of who owes whom.

c. 1200 BCE

Cowrie shells

Shells pass as money in China, and later across Africa and South Asia. The character 貝, "shell", still sits inside Chinese words for wealth and trade.

c. 600 BCE

The first coins

Lydia, in today's Türkiye, strikes lumps of electrum with a lion stamp. The stamp is a state's promise of weight and purity, so no one has to weigh it.

1020s CE

Paper money

Song-dynasty China issues the jiaozi, the first government paper money. Marco Polo later astonishes Europe with reports of it.

1661

Banknotes in Europe

Stockholms Banco prints Europe's first banknotes, issues far too many, and collapses within a few years. A lesson in scarcity.

1816

The gold standard

Britain ties the pound to a fixed weight of gold; by the 1870s most trading nations follow. A note is now a claim on metal in a vault.

1950

The card

Diners Club issues the first general-purpose charge card. Paying becomes a signature, and later a tap, instead of a handover of notes.

1971

Fiat money

The United States ends the dollar's link to gold. Since then nearly all money is fiat: valuable because a state demands it in taxes and people accept it.

2009 → today

Money as code

Bitcoin's first block is mined; mobile money and instant payments spread. Most money is now an entry in a database, moved by tapping a screen.

Chapter 5Where money comes from

Who makes it?

Two kinds of institution. One prints the notes you can see. The other creates most of the money you cannot.

The central bank (the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England and their peers) issues notes and coins and creates reserves, the deposits that commercial banks hold with it.

Commercial banks create most of the rest. When a bank lends you $10,000, it does not hand over another customer's savings. It types a new $10,000 deposit into your account. Repaying the loan deletes that money again.

The textbook picture of this chain reaction is the money multiplier. A deposit is partly kept back and partly lent out; the loan is spent and lands in someone else's account; that bank lends again. Drag the slider to see how far one $100 note can go.

Modern banks are limited less by a reserve ratio than by capital rules, risk and the demand for loans. The chain reaction is real; the neat fraction is a simplification.

Chapter 6Why money loses value

The shrinking basket

Money is only worth what it buys. When prices rise across the whole economy, each unit buys less. That is inflation.

What $100 buys today: 20 apples

$55 of today's buying power

In 20 years at 3% inflation, $100 buys what $55 buys today. Prices double about every 23 years.

Buying power of $100 over 40 years, at the rate you chose

Mild inflation, around 2% a year, is what most central banks aim for: enough to keep prices moving, not enough to notice day to day. Even so, it halves the value of cash kept under a mattress in about 35 years.

Runaway inflation destroys money's third job, storing value. In Germany in late 1923 prices doubled about every four days; in Zimbabwe in late 2008, about every day. People stopped accepting the currency, and money stopped being money.

Chapter 7Money today

Mostly numbers, barely any paper

Ask where your money is and the honest answer is: in a database. Cash is now the smallest part of the money supply.

Notes and coinsBank deposits

In the United States, roughly one dollar in ten exists as notes and coins; the rest is deposits, which are promises a bank makes to pay you. In the United Kingdom and Sweden the cash share is smaller still.

Bank deposits are money because everyone treats them as money: your employer pays into them, your landlord accepts them, the tax office demands them. The paper was only ever a convenient way to carry the agreement around.

So, in one sentence

Money is a shared memory of who owes what to whom, made portable, countable and spendable.

  1. Money exists to fix barter's matching problem: with it, you only need to find someone who wants what you sell.
  2. It does three jobs at once. It is a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value.
  3. Good money is durable, portable, divisible, uniform, scarce and, above all, accepted.
  4. It has been cattle, shells, metal, paper and now mostly numbers in bank computers.
  5. Banks create most of it by lending, and inflation slowly eats it, so its value depends on discipline.
  6. Underneath every form is the same thing: trust. We accept it because everyone else does.
